## Top Anime

### Attack on Titan (Shingeki no Kyojin)
Humanity lives behind massive walls to protect themselves from man-eating Titans. When the walls are breached, Eren Jaeger vows to destroy every last Titan. What starts as a straightforward action survival story evolves into one of the most complex geopolitical narratives in anime. The basement reveal recontextualizes the entire series and the final season deals with cycles of hatred, freedom vs. determinism, and whether genocide can ever be justified. Constantly discussed because the ending divided the fanbase hard — some see it as a masterful tragic conclusion, others see it as a fumble that undermined Eren's character. The "no I don't want that" moment is either devastating or laughable depending on who you ask. Comparable to Game of Thrones in terms of cultural impact and divisive ending discourse.

### Hunter x Hunter (2011)
Gon Freecss searches for his absent father, who is one of the world's greatest Hunters. The power system (Nen) is widely considered the best in all of anime/manga — it's deeply logical, has hard limitations, and enables creative tactical fights rather than pure power escalation. The Chimera Ant arc is frequently cited as the greatest anime arc ever written — it's a slow burn that examines humanity, empathy, and what it means to be a monster. Meruem and Komugi's relationship is heartbreaking. The main complaint is that creator Togashi is perpetually on hiatus, so the manga may never actually finish. Discussion usually centers on best arc rankings (Yorknew vs. Chimera Ant vs. Greed Island) and which Nen type people would be. Comparable to Fullmetal Alchemist Brotherhood in terms of consistent quality.

### Fullmetal Alchemist: Brotherhood (FMAB)
Two brothers attempt human transmutation to bring their mother back to life, pay a devastating price, and spend the series searching for the Philosopher's Stone to restore their bodies. Essentially a perfect anime — the pacing is tight, the cast is enormous but every character matters, the themes of sacrifice and equivalent exchange are woven into every plot thread, and it sticks the landing. Brotherhood follows the manga faithfully (unlike the 2003 version which diverged into its own story). Perpetually #1 on MyAnimeList, which itself generates discourse about whether it deserves that spot. The common debate is Brotherhood vs. the 2003 version — 2003 has a darker tone and arguably better early pacing, but Brotherhood has the superior overall narrative. Comparable to nothing because it's the benchmark everything else is measured against.

### Death Note
Light Yagami finds a notebook that kills anyone whose name is written in it and decides to become the god of a new world by killing criminals. The cat-and-mouse game between Light and the detective L is one of the most intellectually engaging conflicts in anime. The first 25 episodes are basically perfect psychological thriller. Common take: the series declines significantly after a certain major character event around episode 25, and the second half with Near and Mello doesn't match the intensity of the L arc. Still universally recommended as a gateway anime because it's short, accessible, and requires no familiarity with anime tropes. Discussion frequently centers on "was Light right?" moral debates and at what point he went from anti-hero to villain. Comparable to Breaking Bad in its protagonist moral descent.

### One Piece
Monkey D. Luffy assembles a pirate crew to find the legendary treasure One Piece and become King of the Pirates. The longest-running mainstream anime/manga with 1000+ episodes and counting. World-building is unmatched in the medium — Oda has been planting seeds for reveals that pay off 500 chapters later. The emotional peaks (Water 7, Marineford, Wano) hit harder than almost anything in fiction because you've spent hundreds of hours with these characters. The barrier to entry is the length, which is the most common discussion point — people asking "is it worth it?" (yes) and where to start. Wano arc finale and Gear 5 brought massive new attention to the series. Comparable to nothing in scope — it's its own category.

### Jujutsu Kaisen
Yuji Itadori swallows a cursed finger belonging to the King of Curses, Sukuna, and enters the world of jujutsu sorcerers. The animation quality (MAPPA studio) is absurd — the fight choreography, particularly Gojo vs. Sukuna and the Shibuya Incident arc, is some of the best ever produced. The manga's ending was controversial — author Gege Akutami wrapped things up in a way many felt was rushed. Gojo Satoru became one of the most popular anime characters of the 2020s. Discussion centers on power scaling (Gojo's Infinity, Domain Expansions), whether the ending was fumbled, and the Shibuya Incident being a top-tier arc. Comparable to Naruto in terms of mainstream appeal and battle system complexity.

### Vinland Saga
Thorfinn grows up as a Viking warrior seeking revenge against the man who killed his father, only to confront whether violence can ever lead to peace. Season 1 is a brutal Viking revenge story; Season 2 completely shifts gears into a pacifist farming narrative about atonement and breaking cycles of violence — and it works brilliantly. The Farmland Saga (Season 2) is one of the most emotionally mature arcs in anime history. Thorfinn's character development from rage-fueled child soldier to someone trying to build a land without war is genuinely powerful. Often compared to Berserk in its medieval setting and mature themes, but ultimately charts its own philosophical path.

### Berserk
Guts, the Black Swordsman, survives in a dark medieval world filled with demons and horror after being betrayed by his closest friend Griffith during the Eclipse — one of the most devastating events in manga. Berserk is considered one of the greatest manga ever written. The art by the late Kentaro Miura is unparalleled in detail and composition. The problem: the anime adaptations have never matched the manga's quality (the 1997 anime is decent but limited, the 2016 CGI adaptation is widely considered terrible). Discussion centers on whether it will ever be finished (Miura passed away in 2021, his assistants are continuing it with his notes), the Eclipse as peak fiction, and Griffith as one of the greatest villains ever written. Comparable to nothing — Berserk influenced Dark Souls, and that tells you everything about its tone.

### Mob Psycho 100
Shigeo "Mob" Kageyama is the most powerful psychic in the world but just wants to be a normal, good person. Created by ONE (the same person behind One Punch Man), it's a coming-of-age story disguised as an action comedy. The animation by Studio Bones is wildly creative and expressive. The core message — that psychic powers don't make you special, and self-improvement through genuine effort is what matters — is delivered with sincerity that never feels preachy. Season 2's finale is one of the most emotionally satisfying episodes in anime. Often overlooked next to One Punch Man but widely considered the superior work by those who've seen both. Comparable to One Punch Man in premise deconstruction but much more emotionally resonant.

### Chainsaw Man
Denji is a broke, exploited teenager who merges with his chainsaw devil pet to become Chainsaw Man. Creator Tatsuki Fujimoto writes with a punk rock energy — the story is chaotic, violent, darkly funny, and surprisingly emotional. The anime adaptation by MAPPA divided fans because it toned down the manga's manic energy in favor of a more cinematic, grounded approach. The opening and ending themes are all different every episode, which was a unique choice. Part 2 of the manga is ongoing and gets increasingly unhinged. Discussion centers on whether the anime adaptation did the manga justice, Makima as a character, and how Fujimoto subverts shonen tropes. Comparable to a Tarantino film adapted into anime — stylish violence with genuine emotional core.

### Demon Slayer (Kimetsu no Yaiba)
Tanjiro's family is slaughtered by demons and his sister Nezuko is turned into one. He becomes a demon slayer to find a cure. The plot is straightforward shonen, but the animation by ufotable is genuinely the best in the industry — the Mugen Train arc and the Entertainment District arc fights are jaw-dropping. It's the highest-grossing anime film of all time (Mugen Train). The common criticism is that the story and characters are relatively simple compared to peers like Hunter x Hunter or FMAB — it's carried hard by animation quality and emotional moments. Discussion usually involves animation clips going viral, Zenitsu being annoying vs. lovable, and comparing ufotable's work to other studios. Comparable to a Marvel movie — high production value, accessible, crowd-pleasing, not necessarily the deepest.

### Steins;Gate
Okabe Rintaro, a self-proclaimed mad scientist, accidentally discovers time travel via a modified microwave and must navigate the consequences. Starts as a quirky comedy about nerds in a lab, then hits a pivot point around episode 12 where it becomes one of the most gripping thrillers in anime. The time travel mechanics are internally consistent and based (loosely) on real theoretical physics concepts. The relationship between Okabe and Kurisu is one of the best-written romances in anime. The common warning: the first 10-11 episodes are slow and some people drop it before the payoff — this is a mistake. Every "boring" detail in the first half is setup that pays off. Comparable to a Christopher Nolan film — cerebral, time-based, rewards patience and attention.

### Code Geass
Lelouch obtains the power of Geass — absolute obedience from anyone he makes eye contact with — and uses it to lead a rebellion against the Britannian Empire occupying Japan. It's a mecha anime that's really a psychological chess match between Lelouch and his childhood friend Suzaku, who fight on opposite sides. The ending is widely considered one of the best in anime history — the Zero Requiem is a masterclass in bringing every thematic thread together. The show is unapologetically dramatic and sometimes melodramatic, but it commits so fully that it works. Discussion centers on Lelouch vs. Light Yagami comparisons, the ending's brilliance, and CLAMP's distinctive character designs. Comparable to Death Note in the genius protagonist using supernatural powers strategically.

### Cowboy Bebop
Spike Spiegel and the crew of the Bebop are bounty hunters drifting through a jazz-infused space Western setting. Each episode is self-contained with its own genre (noir, horror, comedy, action) but the overarching story about Spike's past slowly builds to one of the most iconic endings in anime. The soundtrack by Yoko Kanno is legendary — "Tank!" might be the most recognizable anime opening ever. It's only 26 episodes and a movie, so there's no filler excuse. The Netflix live-action adaptation was cancelled after one season and is generally considered a case study in why live-action anime adaptations struggle. Discussion usually involves whether Spike is alive or dead at the end, best episode debates, and the soundtrack. Comparable to Firefly in tone and "space crew found family" dynamics.

### Neon Genesis Evangelion
Shinji Ikari is forced to pilot a giant mecha (Eva) to fight otherworldly beings called Angels, while dealing with crippling depression, an absent father, and the weight of humanity's survival. On the surface it's a mecha anime; underneath it's a psychological deconstruction of the mecha genre and a deeply personal exploration of depression, loneliness, human connection, and the fear of being known. The original ending (episodes 25-26) is famously abstract and divisive. The End of Evangelion film provides an alternate, more visceral ending that's simultaneously horrifying and beautiful. The Rebuild films (2007-2021) retell/continue the story and the final film provides genuine closure. Discussion is infinite — what the symbolism means, which ending is better, whether Shinji is relatable or insufferable, and Asuka vs. Rei debates that have raged since 1995. Comparable to nothing; it redefined an entire medium and every mecha anime since exists in its shadow.

## Top Shows

### Breaking Bad
Walter White, a high school chemistry teacher diagnosed with terminal cancer, starts cooking meth to secure his family's financial future and transforms into a drug kingpin. The most commonly cited "greatest TV show ever made." Bryan Cranston's performance is a masterclass in slow transformation — you watch a sympathetic man become a monster and can pinpoint the exact moments where he could have stopped but didn't. The "I am the one who knocks" and "Say my name" scenes are permanently embedded in internet culture. Common debates: at what point Walter became irredeemable, whether Skyler was right all along (she was), and how it compares to other prestige dramas. The finale "Felina" is considered one of the most satisfying endings in television. Ozymandias is frequently rated the single best episode of any TV show ever.

### Better Call Saul
Jimmy McGill's transformation into Saul Goodman, the criminal lawyer from Breaking Bad. Somehow this prequel/spinoff matches and possibly exceeds Breaking Bad. It's a slower, more character-driven show that rewards patience enormously. The Chuck-Jimmy relationship is Shakespearean. Kim Wexler is one of the best-written characters in television history — Rhea Seehorn was criminally never nominated for an Emmy. The final season's integration with the Breaking Bad timeline is masterfully handled. Common discussion: whether it's better than Breaking Bad (genuine debate with no consensus), the "Chicanery" courtroom scene, and whether Jimmy was always Saul or if circumstances created him.

### Game of Thrones
Adaptation of George R.R. Martin's fantasy novels about noble families fighting for the Iron Throne while an existential threat approaches from the north. Seasons 1-4 are some of the best television ever produced. Seasons 5-6 are good but show cracks. Seasons 7-8 collapsed under the weight of rushing to a conclusion without source material. The series finale is the gold standard for how NOT to end a show — character arcs contradicted, plot threads abandoned, the Night King defeated in a single episode after 7 seasons of buildup. "Who has a better story than Bran the Broken?" is said with ironic disgust. The discourse shifted the entire cultural conversation from "have you seen Game of Thrones?" to "Game of Thrones doesn't exist past season 6." House of the Dragon is the prequel attempting to rehabilitate the brand.

### The Wire
Each season examines a different institution in Baltimore — the drug trade, the port, city hall, education, and the media — and how systemic failure perpetuates itself. David Simon's masterwork is frequently called the greatest television show ever made by critics and writers. It's deliberately paced and demands your full attention — it doesn't hold your hand. Season 4 (the education system) is widely considered the peak and one of the most devastating seasons of television ever produced. The show was underwatched during its original run and gained its reputation through word of mouth and retrospective analysis. Discussion centers on best season rankings, Omar as one of TV's greatest characters, and the show's relevance to real systemic issues.

### Succession
The Roy family, owners of a global media conglomerate, fight each other for control while their aging patriarch Logan plays them against one another. Every character is a terrible person and it's riveting. The dialogue is vicious — the insults in this show are works of art. Jeremy Strong's performance as Kendall Roy is one of the defining acting achievements of the 2020s. The series finale landed perfectly, which is notable given how many prestige dramas fumble the ending. Common discussions: whether Kendall, Shiv, or Roman was the most sympathetic (trick question — none of them), the "boar on the floor" scene, and whether Logan ever loved his children. Comparable to King Lear set in the Murdoch family.

### True Detective Season 1
Rust Cohle and Marty Hart investigate a serial killer in Louisiana across two timelines spanning 17 years. Matthew McConaughey's performance as the nihilistic philosopher detective Rust Cohle is career-defining. His monologues about time being a flat circle and consciousness being a tragic misstep in evolution became instantly iconic. The 6-minute single-take tracking shot in episode 4 is one of the greatest sequences in television history. Important note: Season 1 is the one everyone talks about. Season 2 was poorly received, Season 3 was decent but forgettable, and Season 4 (Night Country) divided audiences. When someone says "True Detective," they mean Season 1. Comparable to Se7en crossed with a Cormac McCarthy novel.

### Arcane
Set in the League of Legends universe, following sisters Vi and Jinx on opposite sides of a conflict between the utopian city of Piltover and the undercity of Zaun. The animation is stunning — Fortiche Studio blended 3D and 2D techniques into something that looks like nothing else. Here's the thing that surprised everyone: you don't need to know anything about League of Legends to love this show. It's a genuinely great story about class warfare, sisterhood, trauma, and the cycle of violence. Jinx's descent is heartbreaking. The Imagine Dragons needle drops actually work. Season 2 concluded the story and maintained the quality. Widely considered the best video game adaptation ever made and proof that game IP can produce real art.

### Invincible
Mark Grayson inherits superpowers from his father Omni-Man, the world's most powerful hero, and discovers his father has a horrifying secret. It's a superhero show that takes the "what if Superman was evil?" premise and builds a genuinely compelling story around it. The end of episode 1 is one of the most shocking reveals in recent animation. J.K. Simmons as Omni-Man delivers a performance that makes you understand a genocidal alien, which is quite a feat. The "Think, Mark!" scene broke the internet. Season 2 expanded the scope significantly. Common discussion: Invincible vs. The Boys as the best mature superhero content, and how it handles the consequences of super-powered violence more honestly than the MCU.

### The Boys
Superheroes are real, they're managed by a megacorporation called Vought, and most of them are terrible people. A group of regular humans fights back. The most savage satire of corporate America and celebrity culture currently on television, disguised as a superhero show. Homelander (Antony Starr) is one of the most terrifying villains on TV — a Superman-level being with the emotional maturity of a neglected child and the ego of a narcissist. The show is extremely graphic and not for everyone. Season 4 leaned harder into political satire and divided some viewers. Common discussion: Antony Starr deserving every award, whether the show is too on-the-nose with its satire, and Homelander vs. Omni-Man comparisons.

### Severance
Employees of Lumon Industries undergo a procedure that surgically separates their work memories from their personal memories. Your "innie" only exists at work and your "outie" only exists outside. The Season 1 finale is one of the greatest episodes of television in the last decade — the tension is unbearable. The show raises genuine philosophical questions about identity, consciousness, and labor exploitation. The visual design is immaculate — Lumon's sterile, retro-futuristic office is deeply unsettling. Ben Stiller directed much of it, which surprises people. Season 2 expanded the mythology while maintaining the claustrophobic tension. Common discussion: theories about what Lumon actually does, the ethics of severance, and whether the innies are slaves.

### Shogun (2024)
Set in 1600 Japan, following the political machinations of Lord Toranaga (inspired by Tokugawa Ieyasu) as he outmaneuvers his rivals for control of Japan, seen partly through the eyes of English navigator John Blackthorne. Hiroyuki Sanada's performance is commanding. The show treats Japanese culture with genuine respect — most of the dialogue is in Japanese with subtitles, which was a deliberate and correct choice. The political intrigue is on the level of early Game of Thrones. It swept the Emmys. Common discussion: comparisons to the 1980 miniseries, appreciation for the authentic cultural portrayal, and debates about whether a second season is needed (the story is largely self-contained).

## Film Discourse

### Essential Films by Genre

**Sci-Fi:** Blade Runner (1982) and Blade Runner 2049, Alien and Aliens, 2001: A Space Odyssey, The Matrix, Arrival, Ex Machina, Interstellar, The Thing, Stalker, Dune (2021) and Dune Part Two. The Blade Runner films are the benchmark for atmospheric sci-fi. 2001 is the benchmark for cerebral sci-fi. The Matrix is the benchmark for action sci-fi. Dune Part Two proved that dense source material can become spectacular cinema.

**Horror:** The Shining, Hereditary, The Exorcist, Psycho, Get Out, The Texas Chain Saw Massacre, Halloween (1978), Midsommar, The Silence of the Lambs, It Follows, The Witch. Hereditary's car scene is the modern equivalent of the Psycho shower scene in terms of audience shock. A24 revitalized horror by treating it as a legitimate artistic genre rather than a disposable one.

**Crime/Thriller:** The Godfather I and II, Goodfellas, Pulp Fiction, Se7en, No Country for Old Men, Heat, The Departed, Zodiac, Sicario, The Silence of the Lambs. The Godfather discourse is eternal — Part I vs. Part II is a genuine debate (Part II has the higher highs, Part I is more consistent). Heat's coffee shop scene between De Niro and Pacino is cinema.

**Drama:** The Shawshank Redemption, Schindler's List, There Will Be Blood, Parasite, Whiplash, 12 Angry Men, Fight Club, American Beauty, The Social Network, Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind. Parasite winning Best Picture was a watershed moment for non-English language films at the Oscars. Whiplash is the movie that makes musicians nod in painful recognition.

**Action:** Die Hard, Mad Max: Fury Road, The Dark Knight, Terminator 2, John Wick, Kill Bill, The Raid, Gladiator, Top Gun: Maverick, Mission Impossible: Fallout. Mad Max: Fury Road is what happens when a 70-year-old director with unlimited vision gets a budget. Top Gun: Maverick proved legacy sequels can work when they respect the audience.

**Animation (Non-Anime):** Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse, The Incredibles, Wall-E, Ratatouille, Toy Story, Shrek (unironically a great film), Up (the first 10 minutes), Inside Out. Spider-Verse changed what people thought animation could look like and every animated film since is measured against it.

### Common Film Discourse Topics

**Practical Effects vs. CGI:** The dominant discourse in film discussion spaces. The consensus position: practical effects age better because they exist in physical space and interact with real lighting. CGI ages poorly when it's used as a shortcut but can be invisible when done well (you don't notice good CGI because it looks real). The Thing (1982), Jurassic Park, and Mad Max: Fury Road are the go-to examples for practical effects superiority. The Star Wars prequels and recent MCU are the go-to examples of CGI overuse. The nuanced take: the best films use both — CGI to enhance practical work, not replace it.

**Marvel Fatigue:** The MCU peaked culturally around Endgame (2019) and has been in decline since. Phase 4 and 5 produced too much content of inconsistent quality, leading to audience burnout. The VFX worker exploitation discourse (crunched timelines, underpaid artists) added an ethical dimension. Guardians of the Galaxy Vol. 3 and the Spider-Verse films proved the fatigue is about quality, not the genre itself. The common take: people aren't tired of superhero movies, they're tired of bad superhero movies assembled on a factory line.

**A24 Films:** A24 became the first film studio to function as a brand that audiences follow. Everything Everywhere All at Once winning Best Picture was their coronation. Their horror output (Hereditary, Midsommar, The Witch, X) revitalized the genre. The criticism is that "A24 film" has become a shorthand for a certain aesthetic (slow, atmospheric, ambiguous ending) that can feel formulaic in its own way. But they've consistently championed original filmmaking over IP regurgitation, which earns respect.

**Letterboxd Culture:** Letterboxd became the de facto platform for film discussion, especially among younger audiences. The rating system and reviews have their own culture — half-star reviews that are just jokes, the discourse around what deserves 5 stars, people rating films they haven't seen based on controversy. "Letterboxd review" has become its own content format.

### Director Filmographies People Actually Debate

**Christopher Nolan:** The Dark Knight, Inception, Interstellar, Dunkirk, Tenet, Oppenheimer, Memento, The Prestige. The common criticism: his dialogue can be exposition-heavy and his emotional beats sometimes feel cold or mechanical. The common praise: nobody else working at this scale makes original (non-IP) blockbusters that demand IMAX. Oppenheimer winning Best Picture and Best Director was seen as overdue career recognition. The ranking debate is fierce — The Dark Knight vs. Inception vs. Interstellar vs. The Prestige as his best film is a four-way race. Tenet is his most divisive (ambitious or incomprehensible).

**Denis Villeneuve:** Arrival, Blade Runner 2049, Dune and Dune Part Two, Sicario, Prisoners, Enemy, Incendies. Possibly the most respected director working today in online film spaces. Blade Runner 2049 is considered the rare sequel that matches or exceeds the original. Dune Part Two might be his masterpiece — it proved epic sci-fi can be commercially successful without dumbing down the material. Arrival is the film that made people realize he was operating on another level. The discourse is less about ranking his films and more about when he'll finally get a Best Director Oscar.

**Quentin Tarantino:** Pulp Fiction, Kill Bill, Inglourious Basterds, Django Unchained, Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, Reservoir Dogs, Jackie Brown, The Hateful Eight. His dialogue is immediately recognizable — long, digressive conversations that build tension. The foot thing is a meme but also real. The violence discourse: is it gratuitous or purposeful? (Usually both.) He claims his next film will be his last, which generates its own speculation. Ranking his films is a classic debate — Pulp Fiction is the default answer but Inglourious Basterds has a strong case. Jackie Brown is the "underrated pick" that film buffs champion.

**David Fincher:** Fight Club, Se7en, Zodiac, The Social Network, Gone Girl, Mindhunter (TV), The Game, Panic Room, The Killer. The coldest, most precise director working — every frame is calculated. Fight Club is one of the most misunderstood films ever made (it's a satire of toxic masculinity, not an endorsement). The Social Network is increasingly recognized as the defining film of the internet age. Zodiac is a masterpiece of procedural filmmaking that's more about obsession than the actual Zodiac killer. Gone Girl demonstrated he could make a mainstream thriller without compromising his sensibility. Common discussion: whether Fight Club or The Social Network is his best, and how Zodiac was robbed of recognition on release.

**Hayao Miyazaki / Studio Ghibli:** Spirited Away, Princess Mononoke, My Neighbor Totoro, Howl's Moving Castle, Nausicaa, Laputa: Castle in the Sky, Porco Rosso, Kiki's Delivery Service, The Wind Rises, The Boy and the Heron. Miyazaki is to animation what Shakespeare is to theater — the undisputed master whose work transcends the medium. Spirited Away winning the Oscar for Best Animated Feature was a landmark moment. His films have a warmth and humanism that's impossible to replicate — the way he animates wind, food, and flight borders on spiritual. Princess Mononoke is the gateway for people who think animation is "for kids." The Boy and the Heron (2023) proved that even in his 80s, he's still making deeply personal, challenging work. The discourse is usually about ranking his films and introducing people to Ghibli for the first time — there's a genuine joy in the community around first-time Ghibli watches.
