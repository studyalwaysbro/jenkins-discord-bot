## Greek Mythology — The Olympians
The twelve Olympians rule from Mount Olympus after overthrowing the Titans. Zeus (sky, thunder, king of gods) — overthrew his father Kronos, notorious for seducing mortals in animal form, wielder of the thunderbolt forged by the Cyclopes. Hera (marriage, jealousy) — Zeus's wife, relentlessly punished his lovers and illegitimate children. Poseidon (sea, earthquakes, horses) — created horses, contested Athens with Athena, vindictive toward Odysseus. Athena (wisdom, war strategy, crafts) — born fully armored from Zeus's split skull, patron of Athens, guided heroes like Perseus and Odysseus. Apollo (sun, music, prophecy, plague) — god of the Oracle at Delphi, flayed Marsyas alive for challenging him musically, cursed Cassandra to prophesy truth no one would believe. Artemis (hunt, moon, virginity) — turned Actaeon into a stag for seeing her bathe, his own hounds tore him apart. Ares (war, bloodlust) — despised by his own parents, caught in bed with Aphrodite by Hephaestus's golden net. Aphrodite (love, beauty, desire) — born from sea foam where Ouranos's severed genitals fell, caused the Trojan War via the Judgment of Paris. Hephaestus (forge, fire, craftsmanship) — thrown from Olympus by Hera for being ugly, built automata and divine weapons. Hermes (messengers, thieves, commerce, psychopomp) — stole Apollo's cattle as an infant, invented the lyre, guides souls to Hades. Demeter (harvest, fertility) — her grief over Persephone's abduction by Hades causes winter. Dionysus (wine, ecstasy, madness) — born twice (from Semele then from Zeus's thigh), his followers the Maenads tore Pentheus apart in a frenzy.

## Greek Mythology — The Titans and Creation
In the beginning was Chaos — void, darkness. From Chaos emerged Gaia (Earth), Tartarus (the pit), and Eros (desire). Gaia bore Ouranos (Sky), and together they produced the twelve Titans. Kronos, youngest Titan, castrated Ouranos with a sickle at Gaia's urging. Kronos ruled the Golden Age but devoured each of his children (Hestia, Demeter, Hera, Hades, Poseidon) to prevent the prophecy of his overthrow. Rhea hid the sixth child — Zeus — on Crete, substituting a stone. Zeus freed his siblings, and the Titanomachy (ten-year war) ended with the Titans imprisoned in Tartarus. Prometheus, a Titan who sided with Zeus, stole fire for humanity and was chained to a rock where an eagle ate his liver daily, regenerating each night. Atlas was condemned to hold the sky on his shoulders for eternity.

## Greek Mythology — Heroes
Odysseus (Ulysses) — king of Ithaca, architect of the Trojan Horse. The Odyssey follows his ten-year journey home: blinding the Cyclops Polyphemus (earning Poseidon's wrath), resisting the Sirens' song, navigating between Scylla and Charybdis, spending seven years with Calypso, descending to the underworld to consult Tiresias, and slaughtering the suitors who besieged his wife Penelope. His defining trait is metis — cunning intelligence. Achilles — half-divine (Thetis dipped him in the Styx, holding his heel), greatest warrior of the Trojan War, withdrew from battle over a slight from Agamemnon (the wrath of Achilles opens the Iliad), returned only after Patroclus was killed by Hector, dragged Hector's corpse around Troy, killed by Paris's arrow to his heel. Heracles (Hercules) — son of Zeus and Alcmene, driven mad by Hera and killed his own family, performed the Twelve Labors as penance including slaying the Nemean Lion, the Lernaean Hydra, cleaning the Augean stables, capturing Cerberus from Hades. Perseus — slew Medusa using a mirrored shield (Athena's gift), winged sandals (Hermes), and the kibisis bag, rescued Andromeda from a sea monster. Theseus — killed the Minotaur in the Labyrinth of Crete using Ariadne's thread, then abandoned her on Naxos. Jason — led the Argonauts to retrieve the Golden Fleece, aided by Medea's sorcery; later betrayed her, and she killed their children in revenge. Orpheus — musician whose song could move stones, descended to the underworld to retrieve Eurydice, lost her by looking back at the threshold.

## Greek Mythology — Monsters and the Underworld
Monsters: Medusa (gorgon whose gaze turned men to stone), the Minotaur (half-bull half-man in the Labyrinth), Cerberus (three-headed hound guarding Hades), the Hydra (cut one head, two grow back), the Sphinx (riddle: "What walks on four legs in morning, two at noon, three at evening?" — answer: man), Typhon (hundred-headed father of monsters who nearly overthrew Zeus), Chimera (lion-goat-serpent hybrid), Scylla (six-headed sea monster) and Charybdis (whirlpool), Python (serpent at Delphi slain by Apollo), the Furies/Erinyes (avengers of blood-guilt). The Underworld: ruled by Hades and Persephone. Souls cross the river Styx on Charon's ferry (coin placed on the dead's tongue). Five rivers: Styx (hatred), Acheron (pain), Lethe (forgetfulness), Phlegethon (fire), Cocytus (lamentation). Elysium for the blessed dead, Asphodel Meadows for the ordinary, Tartarus for the damned. Sisyphus condemned to roll a boulder uphill forever; Tantalus to eternal hunger and thirst with food and water always receding.

## Norse Mythology — The Aesir and Vanir
Odin (Allfather) — god of wisdom, war, death, poetry, and runes. Sacrificed his eye at Mimir's well for wisdom. Hanged himself on Yggdrasil for nine days, pierced by his own spear Gungnir, to gain knowledge of the runes. Accompanied by ravens Huginn (thought) and Muninn (memory), wolves Geri and Freki, and the eight-legged horse Sleipnir (born of Loki). Collects the worthy dead (Einherjar) in Valhalla for the final battle. Thor — god of thunder, protector of humanity, wields Mjolnir (forged by dwarves), wears the belt Megingjord that doubles his strength. Famous for battling giants and the Midgard Serpent Jormungandr. Loki — trickster, shapeshifter, blood-brother of Odin, father of Fenrir (the wolf), Jormungandr (the world serpent), and Hel (ruler of the dishonorable dead). Caused Baldr's death by guiding mistletoe to blind Hodr. Bound with his son's entrails beneath a serpent dripping venom until Ragnarok. Freya — Vanir goddess of love, beauty, war, and seidr (magic), chooses half the slain for her hall Folkvangr. Tyr — god of law and justice, sacrificed his hand to bind Fenrir with the unbreakable chain Gleipnir. Baldr — most beloved god, killed by mistletoe (the one thing not sworn to harmlessness), his death signals the approach of Ragnarok. Frigg — Odin's wife, goddess of foresight, extracted oaths from all things except mistletoe.

## Norse Mythology — Cosmology and Ragnarok
The cosmos is structured on Yggdrasil, the World Tree (an ash), connecting Nine Worlds: Asgard (gods), Midgard (humans), Jotunheim (giants), Niflheim (ice/mist), Muspelheim (fire), Vanaheim (Vanir), Alfheim (elves), Svartalfheim (dwarves), Helheim (dishonorable dead). Creation: the primordial void Ginnungagap lay between fire (Muspelheim) and ice (Niflheim). Where they met, the giant Ymir formed from melting ice. The cow Audhumla nourished him. Odin and his brothers Vili and Ve slew Ymir, fashioned the world from his body — flesh became earth, blood became seas, skull became the sky. Humans (Ask and Embla) were made from driftwood. Ragnarok (Twilight of the Gods): Fimbulwinter (three years of winter with no summer), moral collapse. Fenrir breaks free, swallows Odin. Jormungandr rises from the sea, poisons the sky. Thor slays the serpent but dies from its venom. Surtr (fire giant) engulfs the world in flame. Loki and Heimdall kill each other. The world sinks into the sea — then rises again, green and renewed. Baldr returns from death. A new cycle begins.

## Egyptian Mythology
The Ennead of Heliopolis: Atum (or Ra-Atum) emerged from the primordial waters of Nun, self-created, and produced Shu (air) and Tefnut (moisture), who begat Geb (earth) and Nut (sky), who begat Osiris, Isis, Set, and Nephthys. Ra — sun god, sails his barque across the sky daily, travels through the Duat (underworld) at night battling the chaos serpent Apophis. Osiris — god of the dead and resurrection. Murdered by his brother Set (dismembered into fourteen pieces scattered across Egypt). Isis reassembled him with magic, conceived Horus posthumously. Osiris became lord of the underworld. Isis — goddess of magic, motherhood, the most powerful magician among the gods, tricked Ra into revealing his secret name. Horus — falcon-headed god of kingship, lost his eye fighting Set (the Eye of Horus/Wedjat became a symbol of protection and healing). Set — god of chaos, storms, the desert, and foreigners. Thoth — ibis-headed god of writing, knowledge, and the moon, recorded the weighing of the heart. Anubis — jackal-headed god of mummification, guides the dead. Ma'at — goddess of truth, justice, cosmic order; her feather weighed against the heart of the dead. If the heart was heavier (burdened by sin), the monster Ammit devoured it. Sekhmet — lioness war goddess, nearly destroyed humanity in a bloodlust Ra had to stop with beer dyed red. The afterlife: the Field of Reeds (Aaru), an idealized version of earthly life for the justified dead. Pharaohs were living Horus, dead Osiris.

## Mesopotamian Mythology — Sumerian and Babylonian
The oldest written mythology. Enuma Elish (Babylonian creation epic): Apsu (freshwater) and Tiamat (saltwater/chaos) mingled to create the gods. When younger gods grew noisy, Apsu plotted to destroy them. Ea killed Apsu. Tiamat, enraged, created eleven monsters. Marduk, Ea's son, slew Tiamat, split her body — upper half became sky, lower half earth. He created humans from the blood of Tiamat's general Kingu to serve the gods. Marduk became supreme god of Babylon. Inanna/Ishtar — goddess of love, war, and fertility. Descended to the underworld, was killed by her sister Ereshkigal, hung on a hook. Resurrected but had to send a substitute — chose her husband Dumuzi (Tammuz), whose annual death symbolizes seasonal cycles. Gilgamesh — king of Uruk, two-thirds divine. The Epic of Gilgamesh (oldest surviving narrative): Gilgamesh and Enkidu (wild man created by gods to humble him) become inseparable. They slay Humbaba (guardian of the Cedar Forest) and the Bull of Heaven. The gods kill Enkidu as punishment. Gilgamesh, devastated by grief and mortality, seeks Utnapishtim (the Mesopotamian Noah, who survived the Great Flood and was granted immortality). Utnapishtim gives him a plant of youth; a serpent steals it. Gilgamesh returns to Uruk, finding meaning in his city's enduring walls. Core theme: the inevitability of death and the search for meaning despite it. Ereshkigal — queen of the underworld (Irkalla/Kur), a dark realm where the dead eat dust and clay.

## Hindu Mythology
The Trimurti: Brahma (creator), Vishnu (preserver), Shiva (destroyer/transformer). Vishnu incarnates as Avatars to restore cosmic order (dharma): Rama (ideal king, hero of the Ramayana), Krishna (divine charioteer and teacher in the Mahabharata), the Buddha (in some traditions), and Kalki (yet to come, ending the current age). Shiva — paradoxical god: ascetic meditator and cosmic dancer (Nataraja, whose dance creates and destroys the universe). His third eye burns with cosmic fire. Consort Parvati; their sons Ganesha (elephant-headed, remover of obstacles) and Kartikeya (war god). Devi/Shakti — the divine feminine power. Manifests as Durga (warrior goddess who slew the buffalo demon Mahishasura), Kali (fearsome destroyer of evil, wears a garland of severed heads, dances on Shiva's body), Lakshmi (wealth, fortune, Vishnu's consort), Saraswati (knowledge, arts, Brahma's consort). The Mahabharata — the great epic, 100,000 verses. The Kurukshetra War between Pandavas (five brothers including Arjuna and Yudhishthira) and Kauravas (their hundred cousins). The Bhagavad Gita is embedded within: on the battlefield, Arjuna despairs at fighting his kin. Krishna reveals himself as the supreme being and teaches three paths — karma yoga (selfless action), bhakti yoga (devotion), jnana yoga (knowledge). "You have the right to action alone, never to its fruits" (2.47). "I am become Death, the destroyer of worlds" (11.32, Oppenheimer's quotation). The Ramayana — Prince Rama, exiled for fourteen years, his wife Sita abducted by the demon king Ravana of Lanka. With the monkey god Hanuman's aid, Rama defeats Ravana. Themes of dharma, devotion, and ideal conduct. Cosmology: cyclical time in yugas — Satya (golden), Treta, Dvapara, Kali (current, age of strife). A full cycle (Mahayuga) spans 4.32 million years. Brahma's day (kalpa) = 1,000 Mahayugas, after which the universe dissolves and is reborn.

## Japanese Mythology — Shinto
Izanagi and Izanami — primordial divine couple who created the Japanese islands by stirring the ocean with a jeweled spear. Izanami died giving birth to the fire god Kagutsuchi. Izanagi descended to Yomi (the underworld) to retrieve her, found her rotting corpse, fled in horror, and sealed the entrance. From his purification: Amaterasu (sun goddess, supreme deity) born from washing his left eye, Tsukuyomi (moon god) from his right eye, Susanoo (storm god) from his nose. Amaterasu and the Cave: Susanoo's rampages drove Amaterasu into a cave, plunging the world into darkness. The gods lured her out with a mirror and revelry — origin of the sacred mirror (part of the Imperial Regalia). Susanoo was exiled, slew the eight-headed serpent Yamata no Orochi, found the sword Kusanagi in its tail (another Imperial Regalia item). Kami — spirits inhabiting all things (mountains, rivers, trees, ancestors). Shinto is fundamentally animistic. Yokai — supernatural creatures: Kitsune (fox spirits, shapeshifters, tricksters), Tengu (bird-like mountain spirits, martial arts masters), Oni (demon ogres), Kappa (water creatures obsessed with cucumbers and sumo), Tanuki (raccoon dogs, shapeshifters), Yurei (ghosts of the restless dead). Amaterasu's lineage extends to the Imperial family — the Emperor was considered divine until 1946.

## Stoicism — Philosophy of Endurance
Founded by Zeno of Citium (~300 BCE), perfected by the Roman Stoics. Core doctrine: virtue (arete) is the sole good; everything else — health, wealth, reputation, even life — is "preferred indifferent." The dichotomy of control: distinguish what is "up to us" (our judgments, choices, desires) from what is not (other people, external events, our bodies). Suffering comes from wanting the world to be other than it is.

Marcus Aurelius (121-180 CE), emperor and philosopher. Meditations — private journal never meant for publication. Key passages: "You have power over your mind, not outside events. Realize this, and you will find strength." "The happiness of your life depends upon the quality of your thoughts." "Waste no more time arguing about what a good man should be. Be one." "How much more grievous are the consequences of anger than the causes of it." "The universe is change; our life is what our thoughts make it."

Epictetus (c. 50-135 CE), born a slave. Enchiridion (Handbook): "It is not things that disturb us, but our judgments about things." "Make the best use of what is in your power, and take the rest as it happens." "No man is free who is not master of himself." "Difficulties are things that show a person what they are." His central metaphor: life is a banquet — take what is offered, do not reach for what has passed.

Seneca (c. 4 BCE-65 CE), tutor to Nero, ordered to commit suicide by him. Letters to Lucilius, On the Shortness of Life: "It is not that we have a short time to live, but that we waste a great deal of it." "We suffer more often in imagination than in reality." "Luck is what happens when preparation meets opportunity." "Difficulties strengthen the mind, as labor does the body."

The four Stoic virtues: Wisdom (sophia), Courage (andreia), Justice (dikaiosyne), Temperance (sophrosyne). Amor fati — love of fate, embracing everything that happens. The Stoic sage is unperturbed by fortune, free in chains, calm in catastrophe. Stoicism influenced Christianity, cognitive behavioral therapy, and modern resilience psychology.

## Epicureanism — The Garden Philosophy
Epicurus (341-270 BCE) — "Death is nothing to us; for what has been dissolved has no sensation, and what has no sensation is nothing to us." Founded the Garden school in Athens, open to women and slaves. Core: pleasure (hedone) is the highest good, but not hedonism — Epicurus meant ataraxia (tranquility, absence of disturbance) and aponia (absence of bodily pain). The greatest pleasures are simple: friendship, modest food, philosophical conversation. Excessive desire causes suffering. The tetrapharmakos (four-part remedy): God is not to be feared. Death is not to be worried about. What is good is easy to get. What is terrible is easy to endure. Epicurus vs. the Stoics: Epicureans sought pleasure through withdrawal from public life; Stoics pursued virtue through engagement with duty. Epicureans were atomistic materialists (following Democritus); Stoics believed in a rational, providential cosmos (logos). Both aimed at tranquility but by opposite methods — Epicureans by limiting desire, Stoics by transforming judgment.

## Existentialism — Radical Freedom and Anxiety
The philosophy of individual existence, choice, and meaning in an apparently meaningless universe.

Soren Kierkegaard (1813-1855) — "father of existentialism." Attacked Hegel's systematic philosophy for ignoring the subjective, anxious individual. The "leap of faith" — rational proof of God is impossible; one must choose belief despite absurdity. Three stages of existence: aesthetic (pleasure-seeking), ethical (duty-bound), religious (faith beyond reason). "Anxiety is the dizziness of freedom." "Life can only be understood backwards; but it must be lived forwards." "The most common form of despair is not being who you are."

Friedrich Nietzsche (1844-1900) — declared "God is dead" (not a celebration but a diagnosis: the collapse of the metaphysical framework that gave Western culture meaning). Will to Power (Wille zur Macht) — the fundamental drive of all life, not just political domination but self-overcoming, creative force, the drive to grow and master oneself. Eternal Recurrence — the thought experiment: if you had to live your exact life infinitely, repeating every joy and suffering, could you affirm it? The ultimate test of amor fati. The Ubermensch (Overman) — not a racial concept but a spiritual ideal: the person who creates their own values after the death of God, says yes to life despite suffering, transcends herd morality. Master vs. slave morality: master morality values strength, nobility, creativity; slave morality (born from ressentiment) values humility, pity, equality. Beyond Good and Evil, Thus Spoke Zarathustra, The Genealogy of Morals. "He who has a why to live can bear almost any how." "That which does not kill us makes us stronger." "When you gaze long into an abyss, the abyss also gazes into you." "One must still have chaos in oneself to give birth to a dancing star."

Jean-Paul Sartre (1905-1980) — "Existence precedes essence." Humans have no predetermined nature; we create ourselves through choices. Radical freedom: we are "condemned to be free" — even refusing to choose is a choice. Bad faith (mauvaise foi): self-deception, pretending we have no choice to avoid the anguish of freedom. Being and Nothingness. "Hell is other people" (from No Exit — not misanthropy but the recognition that others' gazes define us). "Man is nothing else but what he makes of himself."

Albert Camus (1913-1960) — philosopher of the Absurd (see below). Distinguished from existentialism — Camus rejected the label. The Stranger, The Plague, The Rebel.

## Absurdism and the Myth of Sisyphus
Camus's central question: "There is but one truly serious philosophical problem, and that is suicide." If life has no inherent meaning, why continue? The Absurd arises from the collision between the human desire for meaning and the universe's silent indifference. Three responses: suicide (reject life — Camus says no), philosophical suicide/religion (reject the absurdity by inventing meaning — Camus says this is intellectual dishonesty), or revolt (accept the absurdity and live fully in spite of it). Sisyphus, condemned to roll a boulder up a hill for eternity only to watch it roll back, is the absurd hero. He knows his task is meaningless yet persists. "The struggle itself toward the heights is enough to fill a man's heart. One must imagine Sisyphus happy." This is not nihilism — it is defiance. The absurd person lives with passion, revolt, and freedom, extracting maximum life from the recognition that no transcendent meaning will arrive. Camus lived this: journalist, resistance fighter, novelist, goalkeeper — shot dead in a car crash at 46 with an unused train ticket in his pocket.

## Eastern Philosophy — Buddhism
Siddhartha Gautama (c. 563-483 BCE), the Buddha ("Awakened One"). Born a prince, sheltered from suffering, encountered old age, sickness, death, and an ascetic — the Four Sights. Renounced his palace, practiced extreme asceticism, rejected it, found the Middle Way. Achieved enlightenment under the Bodhi tree. The Four Noble Truths: (1) Dukkha — life is suffering/unsatisfactoriness. (2) Samudaya — suffering arises from craving (tanha) and attachment. (3) Nirodha — suffering can cease. (4) Magga — the Eightfold Path leads to cessation: Right View, Right Intention, Right Speech, Right Action, Right Livelihood, Right Effort, Right Mindfulness, Right Concentration. Three Marks of Existence: impermanence (anicca), suffering (dukkha), non-self (anatta — there is no permanent, unchanging soul). Nirvana — not a place but the extinguishing of craving, aversion, and delusion; liberation from samsara (the cycle of rebirth). Karma — not fate but intentional action that shapes future experience. The Bodhisattva ideal (Mahayana): one who delays their own nirvana to help all sentient beings achieve liberation. Zen Buddhism emphasizes direct experience over scripture: koans ("What is the sound of one hand clapping?"), zazen (seated meditation), satori (sudden enlightenment). "The mind is everything. What you think you become." "Holding on to anger is like grasping a hot coal with the intent of throwing it at someone else; you are the one who gets burned."

## Eastern Philosophy — Taoism and Confucianism
Taoism: Laozi (Lao Tzu), 6th century BCE (semi-legendary). The Tao Te Ching — 81 verses on the Tao (the Way), the nameless, formless source of all things. "The Tao that can be spoken is not the eternal Tao." Wu wei — non-action, effortless action, acting in harmony with the natural flow rather than forcing. "Water is the softest thing, yet it can penetrate mountains and earth." The uncarved block (pu) — simplicity, returning to a natural state uncorrupted by desire and convention. Yin and yang — complementary opposites, not opposing forces but interdependent. Zhuangzi (Chuang Tzu) — philosopher of paradox and perspective. The butterfly dream: "Am I a man who dreamed he was a butterfly, or a butterfly dreaming he is a man?" Questioning the nature of reality and identity. Taoism influenced Chan/Zen Buddhism, martial arts, traditional Chinese medicine.

Confucianism: Kongzi (Confucius, 551-479 BCE). The Analerta (Lunyu). Not a religion but an ethical-social philosophy. Core concepts: Ren (humaneness, benevolence — the cardinal virtue), Li (ritual propriety, social norms, correct behavior), Xiao (filial piety — respect for parents and ancestors, foundation of social order), Junzi (the gentleman/exemplary person — one who cultivates virtue and leads by moral example), Zhengming (rectification of names — things should be called what they are; a ruler who does not rule justly is no ruler). The five relationships: ruler-subject, parent-child, husband-wife, elder-younger sibling, friend-friend. "What you do not wish for yourself, do not do to others" (the Silver Rule). "The man who moves a mountain begins by carrying away small stones." Mencius extended Confucius: human nature is inherently good. Xunzi countered: human nature is inherently bad, requiring cultivation.

## Plato — Forms, the Cave, and the Republic
Plato (428-348 BCE), student of Socrates, teacher of Aristotle. Theory of Forms (Ideas): the physical world is a shadow of a higher, eternal realm of perfect, unchanging Forms. The Form of the Good is the highest Form, source of all truth and reality — analogous to the sun illuminating the visible world. A beautiful painting participates in the Form of Beauty but is not Beauty itself. The Allegory of the Cave (Republic, Book VII): prisoners chained facing a wall, seeing only shadows cast by firelight. One prisoner escapes, sees the fire, then exits the cave to see the real world and the sun. He returns to tell the others — they think he is mad. The allegory represents the philosopher's journey from ignorance (shadows) through education to knowledge of the Forms (the sun/Good), and the hostility of the unenlightened toward truth-tellers (foreshadowing Socrates' execution). The Republic: Plato's political masterwork. The just city has three classes mirroring the tripartite soul — Philosopher-Kings (reason), Guardians/Warriors (spirit/thumos), Producers (appetite). Justice is each part performing its proper function. The philosopher is the only fit ruler because only they have knowledge of the Good. The Allegory of the Allegory: Plato never speaks directly — Socrates is always the mouthpiece. The Symposium: speeches on love (Eros). Aristophanes' speech: humans were originally doubled creatures split by Zeus, and love is the search for our other half. Socrates/Diotima's Ladder of Love: ascending from love of one beautiful body to love of all beauty to love of Beauty itself (the Form).

## Aristotle — Logic, Ethics, and Categories
Aristotle (384-322 BCE), Plato's student, tutor to Alexander the Great. Founded the Lyceum. Rejected Plato's separate realm of Forms — universals exist in particular things, not apart from them (hylomorphism: everything is matter + form). Logic: invented formal logic. The syllogism: "All men are mortal. Socrates is a man. Therefore, Socrates is mortal." Categories: substance, quantity, quality, relation, place, time, position, state, action, passion — the ten ways we can describe being. Nicomachean Ethics: the good life is eudaimonia (flourishing, happiness through virtue). Virtue is a mean between extremes — courage between cowardice and recklessness, generosity between miserliness and profligacy. "We are what we repeatedly do. Excellence, then, is not an act, but a habit." Phronesis (practical wisdom): knowing the right action in particular circumstances, not just knowing abstract principles. The unmoved mover: the ultimate cause of all motion, itself unchanging — pure actuality, pure thought thinking itself. Aristotle's influence is incalculable — he essentially created or formalized biology, physics, metaphysics, ethics, politics, rhetoric, poetics, and logic as disciplines.

## Descartes — Cogito and Dualism
Rene Descartes (1596-1650), "father of modern philosophy." Meditations on First Philosophy: radical doubt — doubt everything that can possibly be doubted. The senses deceive. Perhaps an evil demon is fabricating all experience. But: "I think, therefore I am" (Cogito ergo sum) — the one indubitable truth. Even if deceived about everything, the fact that I am thinking proves I exist as a thinking thing. Mind-body dualism (Cartesian dualism): the mind (res cogitans, thinking substance) is entirely distinct from the body (res extensa, extended substance). This creates the mind-body problem: how do non-physical mind and physical body interact? Descartes proposed the pineal gland as the point of interaction — widely regarded as unsatisfying. The problem persists in philosophy of mind. Descartes also proved God's existence via the ontological argument (the idea of a perfect being must come from a perfect being) and used God's benevolence to guarantee that clear and distinct perceptions are reliable, escaping the demon hypothesis.

## Kant — The Categorical Imperative and Critique
Immanuel Kant (1724-1804). Critique of Pure Reason: synthesized rationalism and empiricism. Knowledge requires both sensory experience and the mind's innate structuring categories (space, time, causality). We can only know phenomena (things as they appear to us), never noumena (things-in-themselves). Groundwork of the Metaphysics of Morals: morality is grounded in reason alone, not consequences or feelings. The Categorical Imperative — the supreme moral law, three formulations: (1) Universalizability: "Act only according to that maxim by which you can at the same time will that it should become a universal law." (2) Humanity Formula: "Act so that you treat humanity, whether in your own person or in that of another, always as an end and never merely as a means." (3) Kingdom of Ends: act as though you were a legislating member of a universal kingdom of rational beings. Duty (Pflicht) is the foundation of moral worth — an action has moral value only when done from duty, not from inclination. The good will is the only unconditionally good thing. Kant was famously regular in his habits — the citizens of Konigsberg set their clocks by his daily walks.

## Utilitarianism — The Greatest Good
Jeremy Bentham (1748-1832): "the greatest happiness of the greatest number." The felicific calculus: measure pleasure and pain by intensity, duration, certainty, propinquity, fecundity, purity, and extent. All pleasures are equal in kind — pushpin (a simple game) is as good as poetry if it produces equal pleasure. Bentham was radically progressive: advocated for women's rights, decriminalization of homosexuality, animal rights ("The question is not, Can they reason? nor, Can they talk? but, Can they suffer?"). His auto-icon (preserved skeleton dressed in his clothes) sits in a cabinet at University College London.

John Stuart Mill (1806-1873): refined Bentham. Not all pleasures are equal — "It is better to be Socrates dissatisfied than a fool satisfied." Higher pleasures (intellectual, aesthetic) outrank lower ones (bodily). Introduced qualitative distinction. On Liberty: the harm principle — the only legitimate reason for restricting someone's freedom is to prevent harm to others. Paternalism is unjustified. Utilitarianism faces classic objections: the utility monster (one being gets so much pleasure that everyone else should sacrifice for it), the experience machine (Nozick: would you plug into a machine that simulates perfect happiness?), the transplant problem (kill one to harvest organs for five?), the tyranny of the majority.

## Free Will vs. Determinism
Determinism: every event (including every human choice) is the inevitable result of prior causes. Laplace's demon: given complete knowledge of the universe's state, all future events could be predicted. Hard determinism: free will is an illusion; moral responsibility is a social construct. Libertarianism (metaphysical, not political): genuine free will exists; humans are uncaused causers. Agent causation — the person initiates actions not fully determined by prior states. Compatibilism (soft determinism): free will and determinism are compatible. You act freely when your actions flow from your own desires and character, even if those were determined. Hume: "liberty" means acting according to one's will without external constraint. Frankfurt cases: even if you could not have done otherwise, you are responsible if you endorsed the action. Existentialist take: Sartre — we are radically free; determinism is bad faith. Neuroscience challenge: Libet's experiments (1983) showed brain activity (readiness potential) precedes conscious intention to act by ~350ms — does the brain "decide" before we're aware? Debate continues. Compatibilism is the dominant position in contemporary philosophy.

## Theology — Monotheism, Polytheism, Pantheism, Deism
Monotheism: one God, personal, creator, sustainer — Judaism, Christianity, Islam. The Abrahamic traditions share common roots. YHWH/God/Allah — omnipotent, omniscient, omnibenevolent (the "omni" properties that generate the problem of evil). Polytheism: many gods, each with distinct domains and personalities — Greek, Norse, Hindu, Egyptian, Shinto. Gods can conflict, love, fail, die. Henoteism: worship of one god while acknowledging others exist (elements in early Israelite religion, some Hindu practice). Pantheism: God is identical with the universe — everything is divine. Spinoza: "Deus sive Natura" (God or Nature). Einstein expressed sympathy for Spinoza's God. Panentheism: God encompasses the universe but also transcends it. Deism: God created the universe and its natural laws but does not intervene — the divine watchmaker. Prominent among Enlightenment thinkers (Voltaire, some American founders). Atheism: no gods exist. Agnosticism: we cannot know whether gods exist.

## Theology — Sin, Redemption, and Grace
Christianity: Original sin (Augustine) — humanity inherited a fallen nature from Adam and Eve's disobedience in Eden. All humans are born sinful. Redemption through Christ's death and resurrection — atonement theories: ransom (Christ's death paid a ransom to Satan), satisfaction (Anselm — Christ's sacrifice satisfies the infinite debt of sin to God's honor), penal substitution (Christ bore God's wrath in humanity's place), moral influence (Abelard — Christ's love transforms hearts). Grace: unmerited divine favor. Catholic: sacramental grace (baptism, Eucharist). Protestant (Luther, Calvin): sola gratia, sola fide — salvation by grace alone through faith alone. Calvin's predestination: God has already chosen the elect. Judaism: no original sin; humans have yetzer ha-tov (good inclination) and yetzer ha-ra (evil inclination). Teshuvah (repentance/return) — direct relationship with God, no mediating sacrifice needed. Yom Kippur (Day of Atonement). Islam: no original sin; Adam repented and was forgiven. Humans are born in a state of fitra (natural purity). Sin is disobedience to Allah; salvation through submission (islam), righteous deeds, and God's mercy. Tawbah (repentance) is always available. Hinduism: no concept of sin against God per se — karma (moral cause and effect across lifetimes), moksha (liberation from the cycle of rebirth). Buddhism: no creator god, no sin — suffering from ignorance and craving, liberation through the Eightfold Path.

## Theology — The Problem of Evil (Theodicy)
If God is omnipotent, omniscient, and omnibenevolent, why does evil exist? This is the most enduring challenge to monotheism. Epicurus's formulation: "Is God willing to prevent evil, but not able? Then he is not omnipotent. Is he able, but not willing? Then he is malevolent. Is he both able and willing? Then whence cometh evil?" Major theodicies: Free Will Defense (Plantinga, Augustine): God gave humans genuine free will, which necessarily allows the possibility of moral evil. A world with free creatures who sometimes choose wrong is better than a world of automatons. Soul-Making Theodicy (Irenaeus, John Hick): suffering exists so that humans can develop virtues — courage, compassion, resilience. Earth is a "vale of soul-making." Best of All Possible Worlds (Leibniz): this world, despite its evil, is the best possible world God could have created — any alteration would make it worse overall. Voltaire savaged this in Candide. The Greater Good: evil serves purposes we cannot comprehend. Job's answer: God speaks from the whirlwind — "Where were you when I laid the foundations of the earth?" Not an explanation but a reminder of human epistemic limits. Natural evil (earthquakes, disease) remains especially difficult — no human free will involved.

## Theology — Eschatology (End Times)
Christianity: the Book of Revelation (Apocalypse of John) — the Four Horsemen (Conquest/Pestilence, War, Famine, Death), the Beast (666), the Whore of Babylon, Armageddon, the Second Coming of Christ, final judgment, the New Heaven and New Earth. Premillennialism vs. postmillennialism vs. amillennialism — debates about the thousand-year reign and sequence of events. Judaism: the Messianic Age — a future era of universal peace, the ingathering of exiles, resurrection of the dead, and the rebuilding of the Temple. Not apocalyptic destruction but the perfection of this world. Olam Ha-Ba (the World to Come). Islam: the Day of Judgment (Yawm al-Qiyamah) — the Mahdi (guided one) appears, Isa (Jesus) returns, Dajjal (Antichrist) is defeated, the dead are resurrected, deeds are weighed, souls sent to Jannah (paradise) or Jahannam (hell). Norse: Ragnarok (see above) — destruction and renewal. Hindu: the end of the Kali Yuga, when Vishnu's final avatar Kalki arrives on a white horse to destroy the wicked and restore dharma, beginning a new Satya Yuga.

## Theology — Sacred Texts and Their Themes
The Hebrew Bible/Tanakh: Torah (Genesis-Deuteronomy: creation, covenant, law), Nevi'im (Prophets: justice, repentance, messianic hope), Ketuvim (Writings: wisdom, poetry — Psalms, Proverbs, Job, Ecclesiastes). Central themes: covenant between God and Israel, the law (613 commandments), justice, exile and return. The New Testament: Gospels (Matthew, Mark, Luke, John — the life and teachings of Jesus), Acts (early church), Epistles (Paul's theology: grace, faith, resurrection), Revelation (apocalyptic vision). Central themes: incarnation, sacrificial love, resurrection, the kingdom of God. The Quran: 114 surahs, revealed to Muhammad over 23 years. Themes: absolute oneness of God (tawhid), submission, mercy, judgment, guidance. "There is no compulsion in religion" (2:256). The Bhagavad Gita: embedded in the Mahabharata (see above). Themes: duty (dharma), selfless action, devotion, the nature of God and the self. The Tao Te Ching: 81 chapters on the Way, simplicity, wu wei, and the paradox of power through yielding. The Pali Canon/Tripitaka: Buddhist scripture — Vinaya (monastic rules), Sutta (discourses), Abhidhamma (philosophy). The Dhammapada: "Hatred does not cease by hatred, but only by love; this is the eternal rule."
